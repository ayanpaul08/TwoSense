The 70% nobody counts: Scope 3 emissions, the carbon hiding in plain sight

When Taylor Swift's Eras Tour rolled through its 2024 stadium run, the headlines fixated on her private jet, and not without reason. In February 2024 alone, jet travel for the tour emitted an estimated 393 tonnes of CO₂, and across the second leg her aircraft logged over 21,000 miles. But the private jet, for all its symbolism, was never the real story.

The real story was the 5.3 million fans. They flew in from other continents. Flight prices into tour cities spiked 50 to 130 percent. Stadiums ran their lights, sound, and air conditioning for hours. Merchandise was manufactured, shipped, bought, and eventually thrown away. None of that appears on any balance sheet Swift's team controls, and yet it dwarfs the jet. By any honest accounting, the overwhelming majority of the Eras Tour's climate footprint sat in its Scope 3 emissions: the indirect, downstream, everybody-else's-problem emissions that almost no organisation counts and almost everyone generates.

The jet was 1% of the story. The fans, the freight, the stadiums and the merch were the other 99%. We only argued about the 1%.

Now scale that pattern from one pop tour to the entire global economy, and you arrive at the single largest blind spot in corporate climate accounting. Whether it is a concert, a cloud computing platform, a bank, or a car company, the same rule holds: the part of the footprint that gets reported is rarely the part that matters.

The AI twist

Here is what makes 2024–26 different. A new force is quietly inflating Scope 3 across the entire economy, and most companies have not realised it is happening to them: artificial intelligence. Every time a company integrates an AI feature, it leans harder on cloud data centres whose energy use is exploding. Microsoft (one of the most climate-committed companies on Earth) saw its total emissions rise 23.4% in a single fiscal year, driven almost entirely by AI and cloud expansion. Its Scope 3 now represents more than 97% of its total carbon impact.

97%
Share of Microsoft's total carbon footprint that sits in Scope 3: the supply chain, hardware, and value-chain emissions outside its direct control. Its Scope 3 has climbed 26% in five years, even as it cut direct emissions 30%. (Microsoft Environmental Sustainability Report, 2025)

The companies buying that AI (the retailer adding a chatbot, the bank deploying fraud detection, the media firm generating content) inherit a slice of those data-centre emissions in their own Scope 3, whether they measure it or not. A 2025 UN analysis found the operational emissions of Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta rose an average of 150% between 2020 and 2023. The carbon cost of the AI boom is being distributed, invisibly, across every company that touches it.

Section 01

Scope 1, 2, and 3, defined plainly

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol (the global standard for corporate emissions accounting) divides emissions into three scopes. The first two are well understood and widely reported. The third is where most of the impact lives, and most of the evasion.

Scope 1
Direct emissions
From sources the company owns or controls: fuel burned on-site, company vehicles, industrial processes.
e.g. A cement kiln burning coal. A company's own truck fleet.
~15%
Typical share of total footprint
Scope 2
Purchased energy
Indirect emissions from the electricity, steam, heating or cooling the company buys and consumes.
e.g. The grid power an office or factory draws.
~15%
Typical share of total footprint
Scope 3
Value chain emissions
Everything else: 15 categories spanning the supply chain upstream and product use downstream.
e.g. The steel in a car. The petrol customers burn. A bank's loans.
~70%
Typical share, far higher for many sectors

The cleanest way to grasp the imbalance comes from a CDP and Boston Consulting Group analysis: for a typical company, Scope 3 emissions are around 26 times larger than Scope 1 and 2 combined. For the retail sector, supply-chain emissions run roughly 92 times operational emissions. When a company reports only Scope 1 and 2, it is describing a sliver of its real impact, and frequently the least consequential sliver.

Section 02

The 15 categories, and the two that get reported

The GHG Protocol splits Scope 3 into 15 categories: eight upstream (your supply chain) and seven downstream (what happens after you sell). The pattern of what companies disclose is revealing.

When companies do report Scope 3, they gravitate to the two easiest categories: Category 6 (business travel) and Category 7 (employee commuting). These are convenient to measure and rarely material. Meanwhile the categories that dominate real footprints (Category 1, purchased goods & services, Category 11, use of sold products, and Category 15, financed emissions) are the ones most often left blank. The reporting follows convenience, not materiality. That gap is the whole game.

Cement is the rare exception: its emissions are overwhelmingly direct, from the chemical process of making clinker. But for a bank, the office footprint is a rounding error against the emissions of everything it lends to. For an oil company, the petrol customers burn is the entire point. The sectors with the most hidden carbon are precisely those that most shape the energy transition.

Tellingly, the gap between disclosure and reality is not uniform across the world: it is a function of regulation. Where rules force the issue, the hidden carbon comes into view:

The EU and UK lead not because their companies are cleaner, but because disclosure is mandatory. The US picture splintered after the SEC withdrew its federal climate rule in 2025, leaving California's economy (the world's fifth largest) as the de facto US standard-setter.
Section 04

The reasons companies skip it, and the closing window

The reasons for low Scope 3 disclosure are partly genuine and partly tactical. Both are becoming less defensible by the year.

The genuine difficulty: Scope 3 requires data from thousands of suppliers across multiple tiers. Most companies lack the systems to collect it, and estimated emission factors carry wide uncertainty. This is real. But the GHG Protocol explicitly permits spend-based screening as a starting point (applying emission factors to procurement spending), which is imprecise but vastly better than reporting zero. Difficulty explains imperfect data; it does not explain blank pages.

The tactical reason: for many companies, honest Scope 3 disclosure would expose net-zero pledges as unachievable. An oil major reporting Category 11 would show a footprint roughly ten times its headline number. The omission is not always an accounting limitation: sometimes it is a strategy. And that strategy is running out of road. The EU's CSRD already mandates Scope 3 for large firms. California's SB 253 requires it from 2027 for ~5,400 companies. The direction of travel is one-way.

The AI accelerant
There is a new urgency. As AI data-centre electricity demand is projected to roughly triple emissions from these facilities (from around 200 million tonnes today toward 600 million tonnes annually by 2030, per Morgan Stanley), every company embedding AI is silently growing its Scope 3. Firms that cannot yet measure their value-chain emissions are about to watch them rise without ever seeing the number.
Section 05

The disclosure rulebook: global and Indian standards

Five frameworks now shape how, and whether, companies must report Scope 3. Understanding which applies to a given company is the first step in judging the credibility of its numbers.

Global Scope 3 disclosure frameworks
FrameworkWho it coversScope 3 requirement
EU CSRD / ESRS 50,000+ large & listed EU companies; non-EU firms with EU operations phasing in from FY2028 Mandatory for material categories, with assurance; double-materiality basis
California SB 253 US public & private firms with >$1B revenue doing business in California (~5,400 firms) Mandatory from 2027 (2026 data); GHG-Protocol based, third-party assured
ISSB (IFRS S2) Global investor-grade baseline; adopted into law by a growing list of jurisdictions Required where adopted; Scope 3 with relief provisions in early years
US SEC climate rule US-listed companies Withdrawn 2025; enforcement stayed indefinitely

India sits earlier on this curve (deliberately so, given the readiness of its corporate base), but the trajectory mirrors the global one.

India Spotlight: SEBI BRSR
A phased approach: strong on Scope 1 & 2, cautious on value chains
India's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework is mandatory for the top 1,000 listed companies. The assured BRSR Core subset covers Scope 1 and 2 comprehensively, but on Scope 3, it currently mandates only Category 6 (business travel) and Category 7 (commuting). The most material categories for Indian industry (purchased goods, use of sold products, and financed emissions) remain outside the mandate. SEBI introduced value-chain disclosure for the top 250 listed entities, but kept it voluntary for FY 2025-26, with no confirmed date for when it becomes compulsory.

The practical upshot for an Indian company with global operations: BRSR data can often be mapped onto CSRD or ISSB requirements, reducing duplication. The firms building value-chain measurement capability now, ahead of the mandate, will avoid the scramble that hit European companies when CSRD arrived. The ones treating SEBI's voluntary window as permission to wait are accumulating a compliance debt that will come due.

Section 06 · The practitioner's guide

Factoring Scope 3 into investment decisions

Scope 3 is not an ESG checkbox: it is financially material information that belongs inside the model, the valuation, the risk framework, and the report. The four domains below show where it fits in each.

A four-part framework
From disclosure
to decision.
Most analysts treat Scope 3 as a reputational data point sitting beside the financials. The firms gaining an edge treat it as an input to the financials: a driver of cost, risk, and terminal value. Four domains, four concrete methods.
01
Modelling
Build a shadow carbon cost into the operating model
Estimate the company's material Scope 3 categories using sector benchmarks where disclosure is missing, then apply a forward carbon price curve (e.g. $50–150/tonne rising over the forecast period). Run it as a line item that grows with revenue. For a high-Scope-3 business (an OEM, a refiner, a bank's financed emissions), this reframes carbon from a footnote into a margin variable.
Do this Add a "Scope 3 carbon exposure" row to the model. Stress it across three carbon-price scenarios. Watch what it does to EBIT in the high case.
02
Valuation
Translate transition risk into the discount rate and terminal value
A company whose product is the emission (Category 11 dominant: oil, ICE vehicles, coal-linked lending) faces structural demand decline as policy tightens. That belongs in a higher cost of capital and a haircut to terminal value, not in a side ESG score. Conversely, a credible Scope 3 reduction pathway can lower perceived risk and support the multiple.
Do this For Cat 11-heavy names, test a 50–150 bps cost-of-capital premium and a lower terminal growth rate. Quantify the valuation delta versus the market price.
03
Risk measurement
Treat missing categories as the risk signal, and benchmark the gap
When a bank omits financed emissions (Cat 15) or an oil firm omits use-of-sold-products (Cat 11), the absence is the finding. Reconstruct the likely exposure with PCAF (for financed emissions) or sector intensity factors, then size it against the balance sheet. For lenders and insurers, unpriced Scope 3 is a forward credit and underwriting risk: stranded-asset exposure waiting to be repriced.
Do this Flag any company omitting its single most material Scope 3 category. Estimate it independently. Map it to potential impairment or repricing risk.
04
Reporting & engagement
Demand targets, not just numbers, and check the base year
A Scope 3 figure without a reduction target is a thermometer, not a thermostat. In engagement, push for verified targets covering material categories, primary-data roadmaps over spend-based estimates, and third-party assurance. Watch for base-year restatements that manufacture progress on paper. In your own reporting, disclose your portfolio's financed Scope 3: increasingly an expectation, not a courtesy.
Do this Score holdings on: material-category coverage, target credibility, assurance level, and base-year stability. Re-test annually.
The bottom line

You cannot manage what you refuse to measure

The Eras Tour argument was always about the wrong number. Fixating on the private jet while ignoring the fans, the freight, and the stadiums is the same error companies make when they report clean Scope 1 and 2 figures and leave the value chain blank. It is the same error an investor makes when they read those figures and stop there.

The trajectory is now unmistakable. The EU's CSRD, California's SB 253, the spread of ISSB, and the forward motion of SEBI's BRSR all point toward comprehensive Scope 3 disclosure becoming the norm. AI is simultaneously inflating value-chain emissions across the entire economy, making the unmeasured suddenly material. The 70% that nobody counts is about to become the 70% that everybody has to. The analysts, investors, and companies that build the capability now will not be the ones scrambling when it does.
