EV or Petrol:
The Unsolved Paradox

Meet Rini

Rini is out of the door by 7:15. Rhea's bag checked twice. Forty-five minutes to Park Circus for the school drop, then the E.M. Bypass crawl to Sector V. By 9:30 she has done 30 kilometres without going anywhere useful. Market near Eco Space on the way home. Roughly 1,200 km a month -- school-gate standstills, flyover queues, signals with no logic.

In March, the receipts started moving. ₹9,000. ₹9,800. Four price hikes in eleven days in May. By the last week of May, she was paying ₹113 a litre -- ₹11,000 a month in fuel, nearly ₹2,500 above budget. Waiting at the pump in New Town, she spotted a newspaper insert for the Tata Nexon EV. "Zero fuel cost," it promised.

Rini is an accountant. She does not take claims like that at face value. What she found was breathless EV enthusiasm or vague charging warnings. Nobody did the maths. So we did.

India's fuel pricing structure defers pain -- it does not absorb it. Which brings back the question everyone had shelved: is it finally time to look seriously at an EV?

The Numbers

What actually decides whether an EV makes sense for you

The answer is never just the fuel price. It is a combination of running cost and owning cost, both shaped by factors specific to your city, your car, and how long you plan to keep it.

Running Cost

What you pay every month

Fuel or charging, service, and insurance. EVs cost significantly less to run -- but the exact saving depends on your city's electricity tariff, petrol price, and monthly km.

Owning Cost

The upfront and end-of-period math

On-road price, resale value when you sell, and whether the battery needs replacing. EVs cost more upfront and lose resale value faster in a thin used-EV market.

Subsidy and Tax

Entirely state-dependent

No central scheme covers private 4-wheelers after FAME-II ended March 2024. Delhi, Maharashtra, Gujarat offer ₹1.5 lakh each. Most other states offer nothing beyond a road tax waiver.

Charging Access

The single biggest variable

Home charging at ₹5 to ₹9 per unit versus commercial chargers at ₹18 to ₹22 changes the economics entirely. If home charging is not an option, the monthly saving drops sharply -- often enough to flip the verdict.

The Infrastructure Reality

The charging reality in 2026 -- honest, not brochure-grade

■
The network grew fast
From 5,000 chargers in 2022 to 39,500 by late 2025. PM E-DRIVE has committed ₹2,000 crore for 72,000 more. On paper, genuine momentum.
■
Quantity without reliability
72% of public chargers face downtime, payment failures, or connector mismatches. India sits at 1 charger per 235 EVs -- the global benchmark is 1 per 10. Range anxiety became charger anxiety.
■
Apartments are the real blocker
No dedicated parking or an RWA that won't approve a charger -- the assumption that makes EV economics work isn't available to a large share of urban India. Guidelines exist. Enforcement does not.
■
Highways are largely solved
91% of national highways have a fast charger within 50 km. Major corridors -- Delhi-Mumbai, Delhi-Bengaluru -- are meaningfully EV-capable. Tier 2 and 3 towns, not yet.

Rini lives in an apartment in Salt Lake. If her society approves a charger, she unlocks ₹8,889 a month in savings. If not, she is on public charging -- saving drops to ₹6,960 and adds inconvenience. The economics still work. The convenience does not.

The Old Objections, Revisited

What kept buyers away -- and what still does.

The Concern Then (2022) Now (2026)
Range anxiety Real. Most EVs offered 200-250 km. Largely resolved for city drivers. Nexon EV gives 340 km real-world.
No chargers on highways Mostly true. Patchy and unreliable. 91% of NH corridors covered. Tier 2/3 still lagging.
Battery replacement cost ₹5-8 lakh. A genuine fear. ₹5-7 lakh for a 45 kWh pack (₹15-20K/kWh market rate). The 8-year/1.6 lakh km warranty means most city buyers will not pay this. But it is real, uninsured risk after warranty.
Too expensive upfront Price gap was ₹6-8 lakh over petrol. Gap narrowed to ₹4-5 lakh. State subsidies exist only in some cities: Delhi ₹1.5L, Maharashtra ₹1.5L, Gujarat ₹1.5L. No central scheme covers private 4-wheelers since FAME-II ended in March 2024.
Resale value Unknown. No used-EV market existed. Still a problem. Real listings show EVs losing 25-35% in year one alone. Used-EV market is thin -- buyers discount aggressively for battery-health uncertainty. Petrol retains 50-55% at 5 years; EVs retain 40-45%. Gap should narrow as market matures.
Apartment charging Near impossible. RWAs refused. Still the biggest unsolved problem for urban India. Now compounded by BIS proposed restrictions on EV charging in residential basements (Oct 2025) and state fire departments citing safety concerns. Legal right exists; practical access does not.
The Honest Verdict

Who should switch now, and who should wait?

Buy the EV if you...
Have dedicated parking with access to a power outlet at home or your apartment society has approved EV charging
Drive primarily within the city and cover 1,000 km or more per month
Live in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, or Chennai where charging density is reasonable
Can hold the car for at least four to five years to reach the break-even on total cost
Are in Delhi, Maharashtra, Gujarat or Assam where state subsidies for 4-wheeler EVs are confirmed (check your state transport department)
Wait if you...
Live in an apartment where the RWA has not approved EV charging installation
Drive long inter-city routes regularly, especially through Tier 2 and Tier 3 towns
Need to change cars within three years -- the break-even has not arrived yet
Are in a city without reliable public charging density and rely on street parking
Have budget anxiety about the upfront gap -- the EMI difference is real and monthly
RupeeSense Verdict
The math works. The infrastructure still has gaps. Your decision lives in between.

The EV wins on economics -- ₹8,889 monthly saving, lower five-year total, ₹1.59 per km vs ₹9.11. The numbers are not close. But they are built on one assumption India's urban housing reality keeps breaking: that you can charge at home overnight.

Before you visit a showroom, answer this honestly: Where will I charge this car on a Tuesday night? Clear answer -- switch. Still figuring it out -- the petrol car is not wrong yet. Just more expensive.
