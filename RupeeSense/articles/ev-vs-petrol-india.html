EV or Petrol:
The Unsolved Paradox

Meet Rini

Rini's mornings have the organised chaos of someone who has memorised every speed breaker between New Town and Park Circus. She is out of the door by 7:15, Rhea's school bag checked twice. The drop near Park Circus takes 45 minutes on a good day -- then the E.M. Bypass crawl back through the city toward Sector V. By the time she sits at her desk it is 9:30 and she has already done 30 kilometres. Evenings: a stop at the market near Eco Space before heading home. She drives roughly 1,200 km a month this way. Not highway kilometres. Kolkata kilometres. No-logic traffic signals, flyover queues, the school-gate standstill at 7:45 every morning.

She first noticed it in March. Her fuel receipts, quietly sitting around ₹8,500 a month, started moving. ₹9,000. ₹9,800. By early May, Kolkata petrol had crossed ₹105. Then four price hikes landed in eleven days. By the last week of May she was paying ₹113 a litre -- her monthly fuel bill had crossed ₹11,000, nearly ₹2,500 above budget. She was at the pump in New Town when she spotted a newspaper insert for the Tata Nexon EV. "Zero fuel cost," it promised.

Rini is an accountant. She does not take claims like that at face value. What she found online was either breathless EV enthusiasm or vague warnings about charging. Nobody just did the maths. So we did.

Brent crude moved 59% from its pre-conflict level. OMCs bled ₹1,700 crore a day. Another ₹2 to ₹5 per litre is being priced in before year-end. India's fuel pricing structure defers pain -- it does not absorb it. Which brings back the question everyone had shelved: is it finally time to look seriously at an EV?

Monthly saving: ₹8,889 -- if Rini charges at home. Kolkata's CESC tariff (₹9/unit) is one of India's highest, which narrows the advantage. On public fast chargers at ₹20/unit, the saving compresses to ₹6,960. Still meaningful, but home charging is the assumption doing all the work.

The EV wins on running cost by a wide margin. The question is whether you can unlock that saving, or whether your living situation locks you out of it.

The Infrastructure Reality

The charging reality in 2026 -- honest, not brochure-grade

■
Public charger numbers have grown fast
From 5,000 chargers in 2022 to 39,500 by late 2025, including 8,400 fast chargers. PM E-DRIVE has committed ₹2,000 crore for 72,000 more. The growth is real.
■
But 72% of public chargers face reliability issues
Ground reports show downtime issues, payment failures, and connector mismatches are widespread. Range anxiety has been replaced by charger anxiety -- arriving at a broken charger. Not a fringe problem.
■
One charger for every 235 EVs
Global benchmark: one charger per 10 EVs. India: one per 235. EV sales are growing faster than the network. Manageable in metros, a genuine constraint elsewhere.
■
Apartment charging is the biggest unsolved problem
No dedicated parking, or an RWA that won't approve a charger -- the assumption that makes EV economics work simply isn't available to a large share of urban India. Guidelines exist. Enforcement is a different story.
■
Highways are getting better, fast
91% of national highways now have a fast charger within 50 km. Delhi-Mumbai, Delhi-Bengaluru -- the major corridors are meaningfully EV-capable. Highway anxiety is largely resolved. Tier 2 and 3 towns, not yet.

Rini lives in an apartment in Salt Lake. If her society approves a charger, she unlocks ₹8,889 a month in savings. If not, she is on public charging -- saving drops to ₹6,960 and adds inconvenience. The economics still work. The convenience does not.

The Old Objections, Revisited

What kept buyers away -- and what still does.

The Concern Then (2022) Now (2026)
Range anxiety Real. Most EVs offered 200-250 km. Largely resolved for city drivers. Nexon EV gives 340 km real-world.
No chargers on highways Mostly true. Patchy and unreliable. 91% of NH corridors covered. Tier 2/3 still lagging.
Battery replacement cost ₹5-8 lakh. A genuine fear. Still ₹3-6 lakh, but batteries now warrant 8 years or 1.6 lakh km. Most buyers will not hit this.
Too expensive upfront Price gap was ₹6-8 lakh over petrol. Gap narrowed to ₹4-5 lakh. Subsidies further reduce this for eligible buyers.
Resale value uncertainty Unknown. Nobody was selling used EVs yet. Nexon EV retaining 60-65% after 3 years, comparable to petrol.
Apartment charging Near impossible. RWAs refused. Still the biggest unsolved problem for urban India.
The Honest Verdict

Who should switch now, and who should wait?

Buy the EV if you...
Have dedicated parking with access to a power outlet at home or your apartment society has approved EV charging
Drive primarily within the city and cover 1,000 km or more per month
Live in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, or Chennai where charging density is reasonable
Can hold the car for at least four to five years to reach the break-even on total cost
Are eligible for FAME-II or state subsidies (check your state's transport department)
Wait if you...
Live in an apartment where the RWA has not approved EV charging installation
Drive long inter-city routes regularly, especially through Tier 2 and Tier 3 towns
Need to change cars within three years -- the break-even has not arrived yet
Are in a city without reliable public charging density and rely on street parking
Have budget anxiety about the upfront gap -- the EMI difference is real and monthly
RupeeSense Verdict
The math works. The infrastructure still has gaps. Your decision lives in between.

The EV wins on economics -- ₹8,889 monthly saving, lower five-year total, ₹1.59 per km vs ₹9.11. The numbers are not close. But they are built on one assumption India's urban housing reality keeps breaking: that you can charge at home overnight.

Before you visit a showroom, answer this honestly: Where will I charge this car on a Tuesday night? Clear answer -- switch. Still figuring it out -- the petrol car is not wrong yet. Just more expensive.
